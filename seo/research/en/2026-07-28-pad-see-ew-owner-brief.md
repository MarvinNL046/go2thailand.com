## Required content

1. Direct definition and Thai script `ผัดซีอิ๊ว`, explaining that the name refers to soy-sauce stir-frying.
2. Taste compass: savoury, lightly sweet, soy-led, charred when the wok allows, normally less chilli-led than Pad Kee Mao.
3. Noodle choice: fresh wide rice noodles as the familiar form, with careful acknowledgement that vermicelli and egg-noodle variants exist.
4. Ingredient signals: `sen yai`, Chinese broccoli/gai lan, egg, garlic, protein/tofu, light/black soy, oyster sauce, fish sauce and chilli vinegar.
5. Sauce and dietary boundary: recipe and vendor formulas vary; rice noodles do not make the completed dish automatically gluten-free, vegetarian or vegan.
6. Choice cards for street/food-court wok, restaurant and home/class rather than fixed restaurant rankings.
7. Three-step order flow: protein, sauce/dietary checks and condiments/heat.
8. Focused comparison to Pad Thai and Pad Kee Mao without taking over the whole comparison cluster.
9. High-heat, single-portion sequence without promising mandatory wok hei on every stove.

## Claim boundaries

- Do not call one fixed sauce blend traditional everywhere.
- Do not claim dark soy sauce is always wheat-based; label and producer determine gluten ingredients.
- Do not call rice noodles proof of coeliac safety because sauces, marinades and shared woks matter.
- Do not promise the dish is entirely non-spicy; the base is usually not chilli-led, but condiments and kitchens vary.
- Do not claim every plate contains meat, egg, oyster sauce, fish sauce or one vegetable.
- Do not claim mandatory wok hei or that a home stove must reproduce restaurant flame.
- Do not publish fixed calories, nutrition, prices, serving sizes or “healthiest/unhealthiest” labels.
- Do not assign a single inventor or exact origin date; describe documented Thai-Chinese/Teochew influence with source attribution.
- Do not use Recipe schema without independently tested quantities, yield and timings.

## Fact, dietary and safety boundaries

- Hoi/Hoy refers to shellfish and tod/thot to frying. Menu spelling varies; the visible shellfish and cooking style matter more than transliteration.
- Mussels are common in familiar Thai Hoi Tod; oyster versions are also established. Both are molluscs, and the kitchen must identify which one is used.
- The dish is more than an egg omelette: a starch or flour batter creates crisp, lacy or chewy structure around shellfish and egg.
- Crisp Hoi Tod and soft/gooey Or Suan overlap in ingredients but are different texture orders. Ask for the preferred style rather than treating poor crispness as the only explanation.
- Phuket Oh Tao is a separate local owner associated with small oysters, taro and its own toppings/texture system; do not collapse it into Hoi Tod.
- Egg and mollusc are direct allergens. Batter, seasoning, soy/oyster/fish sauce, chilli sauce and shared griddle can add wheat/gluten, soy, fish, crustacean, sesame or other exposures.
- It is not vegetarian or vegan. A shellfish-free egg pancake is a different dish; an egg-free, shellfish-free version needs an independently named batter and clean griddle.
- Shellfish may fit some halal interpretations, but frying fat, seasoning sauces, alcohol-containing additions and shared griddle contact vary. Halal suitability requires vendor or certification-level verification.
- The base dish is often mild or savoury; dipping sauce or added chilli can supply heat. Never assign one universal spice level.
- Shellfish and egg must be handled and cooked safely. Crisp edges do not prove the centre or shellfish reached a safe endpoint; use a complete tested method and current official guidance.
- Never publish a fixed price, calorie total, health verdict, automatic dietary status, permanent restaurant ranking, universal heat/crispness, exact storage time, compulsory shellfish species or one authentic formula.

## Fact, dietary and safety boundaries

- Pork belly is the stable protein signal. Pork shoulder or a meatier blend may appear in an adaptation, but a chicken or plant-based braise is no longer proof of classic Moo Hong.
- Soy sauce and dark/black soy drive colour, salt and umami. Soy is an allergen and many soy sauces contain wheat; oyster sauce or stock can add shellfish, fish, wheat or further soy in some kitchens.
- Palm or brown sugar supplies sweetness and glaze, but the dish should not be reduced to `sweet pork`; reduction, pepper, garlic and soy also matter.
- Black pepper, garlic and coriander root/stems form a documented aromatic system. Star anise and cinnamon occur in some versions and are absent in others.
- Moo Hong is usually rich and gently peppery rather than chilli-led. A chilli side or neighbouring curry does not prove the braise itself is hot.
- Moo Hong contains pork and therefore is not halal. A pork-free `hong` adaptation needs its own ingredients and handling and should not be presented as classic Moo Hong.
- Rice is naturally gluten-free, but soy sauce, oyster sauce, stock, shared pots, ladles and sides can prevent the finished meal from being safe for coeliac disease.
- Long braising creates tenderness, not automatic safety. Raw-pork separation, complete cooking, controlled hot holding, prompt chilling and steaming-hot reheating remain direct checks.
- Never publish a fixed price, calorie total, health verdict, automatic gluten-free status, permanent best restaurant, universal cooking time, exact origin date or one authentic spice list.

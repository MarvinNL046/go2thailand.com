## Fact, dietary and safety boundaries

- Pla means fish and pao/phao means grilled or roasted. Pla Pao is not Pla Ra, the fermented-fish seasoning, and not Pla Goong Pao.
- Tilapia/red tilapia and snakehead are documented choices; sea bass and other firm whole fish can appear. Species, farming/wild source, size and availability vary.
- A coarse salt coating protects the skin/scales and helps hold moisture around the whole fish. Some documented methods bind salt with flour or another liquid, so gluten cannot be inferred from appearance.
- The crust, scales and often the skin are lifted away to reach the flesh. Do not recommend eating handfuls of salt crust.
- Lemongrass and kaffir/makrut lime leaves are familiar cavity aromatics, not proof of one universal stuffing.
- The fish flesh is usually mild, smoky and aromatic; the seafood/jaew-style dipping sauce can be hot, sour, salty or sweet and may contain fish sauce, soy, peanut or packaged seasoning.
- Miang Pla Pao adds lettuce or herbs, rice noodles or rice and toppings for wrapping. Every tray component and shared utensil needs its own allergen check.
- Pla Pao is pescatarian only when the complete sauce, coating and grill fit that definition. It is not vegetarian or vegan. Fish may fit many halal diets, but sauce, flour mix, alcohol-containing additions and shared grill vary.
- A whole fish contains fine and large bones. Serve flesh from one side, expose the spine, then lift remaining flesh; children and anyone with swallowing difficulty need particular care.
- Salt crust colour, flaking or time alone does not prove doneness. Use a complete tested method and current official whole-fish guidance; flesh should be opaque and separate easily, with the thickest area checked.
- Never publish a fixed price, calorie total, health verdict, automatic dietary status, permanent restaurant ranking, universal saltiness/heat, exact cooking/storage time, compulsory fish species or one authentic formula.

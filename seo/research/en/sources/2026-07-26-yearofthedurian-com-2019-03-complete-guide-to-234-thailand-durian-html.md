# Updated Guide to 234 Thailand Durian Varieties

March 6, 2019 [30 Comments](https://www.yearofthedurian.com/2019/03/complete-guide-to-234-thailand-durian.html)

Note: Updated June 2, 2024. I keep adding more. Also, I finally re-organized this list in alphabetical order (Roman script). That was a thing.

This article was originally posted in 2013.

Whenever I see news articles confidently state that there are 234 Registered Durian Varieties in Thailand, I have myself a little chuckle.

I’m honored, really I am. Whenever I see the number “234 Durian Varieties” it means the author of that article read this blog post. But there is nothing Official or Scientific about it. It’s a number this yours truly made up about 13 years ago.
### How I Compiled This List of Durian Varieties

The number “234” comes from the original version of the page you’re reading now when I squished together several English-language lists of durian varieties snitched from libraries and begged from exasperated agricultural officers. Some of the lists had 81 varieties, others 173 or 179 varieties.

The most complete list I found had 245 varieties, but 11 of them were repeats. Doing the maths, 245-11=234, making for the magical 234 varieties I originally listed in this article and which has now been confidently proclaimed to be “Registered Durian Varieties.”

This is not correct, and I apologize for leading everyone astray.

Thailand’s Plant Protection Burueau does have a variety registration system, but there are only 13 durians listed there. You can search for them on the [Department of Agriculture website.](http://www.doa.go.th/pvp/?page_id=478)

The much larger list is compiled from germplasms sprinkled around the country and over time. The hunting of these lists was just one challenge.

The other was the creative and ever-changing transliteration of durian names from Thai script to Roman script meant that it was sometimes difficult to tell if I was looking at two completely different durians or one durian with two completely different spellings.

I finally had to give in and learn to read Thai script. I’m not kidding, I learned to read Thai for this blog post. So definitely leave me a comment if you appreciate this post!

### The Families of Thailand Durian

Thai Durian Variety Lists are usually organized into by groups or “Durian Family Trees” rather than being alphabetical order. These durian groups are believed to have originated from the seeds of the same great-grandma tree. You will usually find them arranged into 5 Families, plus a “Miscellaneous” category.

Kob (also Gob, Cop, Kop, etc.) This is a family of over 50 durian varieties that are all believed to be descendents of Kob Mae Thao. Several members of this family have emigrated to Australia, Hawaii and Philippines where they are just known as “Kob” or “Gob” – but what their full name was has been forgotten.

Luang A smaller family with just 10 varieties, Luang includes the famous Chanee as well as the delicious Chomphu Si.

Kanyao (also Kanyao) Thailand’s most expensive durian variety is actually just one in a family of 7 or 8 durians.

Thong Yoi includes the wonderful Nokyib.

The Kampan Family (sometimes translated to Gampan or Gumpun) also emigrated to Australia, but again, lost its last name in the naturalization process. similar manner to Kob, Gampan has taken off in Australia and is probably more common there than in Thailand. Monthong is sometimes classed in this family, as is Chai Ma Fai.
### How to Taste Thai Durian Varieties

Nowadays, Thailand is very focused on the export trade. This has severely reduced biodiversity and led some historic varieties to become almost extinct, as most people will grow the durians that make the most money – currently Monthong, Musang King and Black Thorn.

Above 90% of all durian in Thailand is are Monthong and Monthong alone.

### 17. Chok Loi จอกลอย

[Suan Ban Rao, 2018](https://www.yearofthedurian.com/2016/06/16-thai-durian-varieties-rayong-thailand.html)

[Rajamaitri Farm, 2019](https://www.yearofthedurian.com/2019/05/chanthaburi-history-durian-chok-loi.html)

This petite personal-sized durian is named for a type of small, bulbous water plant. Each durian usually weighs in at under 1kg, but with enormous, bulbous pods that have little seeds hidden within the mound. The texture is thick and clotted with a milk chocolatey aroma. No wonder this one became a local favorite in Chanthaburi!

This variety originates on the farm of the famous Luang Rajamaitri, a local celebrity in Chanthaburi who was born in 1876 and passed away in 1958. We don’t know exactly when this durian was named, but the original tree is at least 100 years old and is still alive and well, growing on the original property.

SW Garden

[Suan Ban Rao](https://www.yearofthedurian.com/2016/06/16-thai-durian-varieties-rayong-thailand.html)

E-nak translates as “you’re heavy” — which is apparently a somewhat mean way to tell someone “you’re fat” in Thailand.

An elongated durian with small, closely clustered thorns, E-nak is really big. The flesh is pale yellow with the same watery/margerine texture as Monthong. The pods seem to form into elongated, slightly flat shape. Had some signs of calcium deficiency on the seeds (burn spots).

Tasting notes: Similar to Monthong in taste, but not as sweet. It seems possible that E-nak is the grandparent tree of Monthong.

### Kob Pikul or Kob Pikun กบพิกุล

Where: [I’m Durian](https://www.yearofthedurian.com/2018/05/a-bangkok-durian%E2%80%A6aurant-im-durian.html), 2018

Kob Pikul is one of my favorite durians. When fully ripe it’s a gorgeous orangy-gold, with the same egg-custard of Chanee but with more vanilla.

The pronunciation of the word “Pikul” is tricky, because in the Thai language, if an “L” comes at the end of the word, it becomes an “N” sound. So while this durian’s name is spelled “Pikul” it should be pronounced like “Pikun.” Yay for phonetics.

Kob Pikul has an elongated, pointy “nose.” It’s name is the same as a pointy origami-like [Bullet Flower](https://www.google.com/search?q=bullet+flower+thailand&rlz=1C5CHFA_enUS817PH818&source=lnms&tbm=isch&sa=X&ved=0ahUKEwigg4Gpx9jgAhULtI8KHX0ACJAQ_AUIDigB&biw=1440&bih=725) common in Thai carvings and decorations, but it’s actually named for man. As in a real person, who’s name was Mr. Pikul.

Mr. Pikul lived in Nonthaburi, and he is the grandfather of Pang at the Durian Conservation Center.

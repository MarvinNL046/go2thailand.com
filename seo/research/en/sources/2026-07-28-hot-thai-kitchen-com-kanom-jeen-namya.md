# Thai Rice Noodles with Fish Curry (kanom jeen namya)

Kanom Jeen Namya ขนมจีนน้ำยา is a dish I grew up on because it's a specialty of Southern Thailand, my hometown. An aromatic fish curry is poured over tender rice noodles, and then served with crunchy veggies. It's healthy and bursting with aromas of Thai herbs such as lemongrass, galangal and grachai. It's also one of the easiest Thai curry dishes to make, with ways to prep ahead to make weeknight dinners even easier!
## What is Kanom Jeen Namya ?

Kanom jeen ขนมจีน (also written as khanom jeen ) is a category of dishes that involves fresh, round rice noodles with some kind of sauce poured on top. I like to call kanom jeen "Thai spaghetti" because the spaghetti-shaped noodles can be paired with various types of sauces, just like you can with Italian spaghetti!

The term kanom jeen refers to this specific type of rice noodles, and for our recipe the word namya refers to the type of sauce. So you can have other types of kanom jeen, for example, a popular one in central Thailand is kanom jeen gaeng kiew waan, which uses [green curry chicken](https://hot-thai-kitchen.com/green-curry-new-2/) as the sauce, and in northern Thailand, one of their iconic dishes is [kanom jeen nam ngiew](https://hot-thai-kitchen.com/nam-ngiew/) which comes with a tomato and pork broth.

Namya is one of the most popular sauces that originated in the South of Thailand, where I grew up. So, I have been eating kanom jeen namya ever since I was a little kid and it occupies a very special place in my heart, indeed. Unfortunately, kanom jeen namya is not easy to find in Thai restaurants overseas, but thankfully, it is super easy to make at home!
## Fun Fact

Kanom jeen noodles can sometimes be fermented which makes them a little tangy - basically a sourdough version! The most common version in Thailand is not fermented, as it is easier to make, but don't pass up the fermented version if you come across them!
## Ingredients and Important Notes

Making kanom jeen namya is easy. The only part that may trip you up is getting the right ingredients, so here I hope to answer any questions you have about them! For amounts, see the full recipe card below.

Dry Vietnamese rice vermicelli, size small or medium. Although in Thailand kanom jeen noodles are fresh, overseas we have to use the dried ones. They actually work great! Counterintuitively, you have to get Vietnamese rice vermicelli, not Thai ones (odd, I know) because what's labelled as "Thai rice vermicelli" are a different kind of noodles that happen to also be translated into the same English word. Vietnamese vermicelli come in various sizes; my preference is for small or medium ones.

You can also substitute Japanese somen noodles. They are actually wheat, but they taste very similar to kanom jeen in the final dish. I've also experimented with soba and udon noodles, and they both actually taste great, albeit different!

Mild dried chilies, such as guajillo, puya, or California chilies.

Spicy dried chilies, optional, to taste. You can make this dish mild or spicy, and the heat level can be adjusted by adding a few or a lot of spicy dried chilies. Spicy chili powder or flakes also work here.

White fish filet. The fish will be pounded and mixed into the sauce to thicken the sauce. So use any kind of inexpensive white fish available to you that has a mild flavour. My preference is for tilapia.

Lemongrass, bottom half goes into the curry sauce as it is the most flavourful half. Save the weaker top half for poaching the fish. Read [more about lemongrass here](https://hot-thai-kitchen.com/lemongrass-101/).

Galangal, thinly sliced. Fresh or frozen is fine. Read [more about galangal here.](https://hot-thai-kitchen.com/galangal-101/)

[Fingerroot or krachai](https://amzn.to/4d0RU4Q) in Thai (pic below). This is the key herb that gives kanom jeen namya its iconic flavour. Fresh or frozen ones are harder to find, but I have seen them at Southeast Asian grocery stores. Brined ones are more easily available. Brined fingerroot comes either whole or julienned; either one works. They are sometimes labeled as "kachai," "krachai," or confusingly "pickled rhizome" or "pickled galingale" (but it's not actually pickled).

Turmeric root or sub ground turmeric.

[Fermented shrimp paste](https://amzn.to/4elnJaQ) (pic below). This is called gapi in Thai, and it's a thick paste made from fermented small shrimp or krill that adds depth and umami to all Thai curries. It is okay to omit it if you do not have it.

Coconut milk. It's important to use good quality coconut milk for this. My preference is for [Aroy-D in UHT Carton](https://amzn.to/3QTTiyE) and not in cans. See my post on [how to choose good coconut milk here.](https://hot-thai-kitchen.com/how-to-make-coconut-milk/)

[Palm sugar](https://amzn.to/4el3xps) or sub light brown sugar or white sugar.

Fsh sauce

[Tamarind paste](https://amzn.to/4cH8IyR). Use cooking tamarind from Thailand which you can get as ready-to-use liquid in a tub or jar, or you can make your own [homemade tamarind paste](https://hot-thai-kitchen.com/making-tamarind-paste/) from pulp. If you want to cook Thai food often, this will come in handy for recipes such as pad thai!

: Kanom jeen typically comes with lots of little accompaniments on the side. Feel free to pick and choose which you want to include, but I recommend the pickles and at least one crunchy veg!

Medium boiled eggs, or cook them to your liking. I boil mine for for 7-8 minutes for jammy yolks that are not runny.

Fresh crunchy vegetables such as bean sprouts, long beans, shredded cabbage, or sliced cucumber.

Pickled mustard greens. (pic below) You can get these in clear plastic bags at Asian grocers. You can also sub other kinds of sour pickles as these really help brighten the dish.

Thai basil or lemon basil leaves
## Step By Step

Here's a bird's eye view of the process. The full instructions are in the recipe card below, and if this is your first time, I hightly recommend watching the full video tutorial to ensure success!

Soak the dried chilies in hot water for 30 minutes to help them blend more easily.

Cut off the top half of the lemongrass (save the bottom for the curry paste), smash them to bruise, cut into pieces and add to a pot. Add 1 inch of water and bring to a simmer. Add the fish filets and poach until they are fully cooked. (in the pic you see other herbs, which I used to do, but I have since streamlined the process and only use lemongrass tops).

Place the fish into a mortar or a large mixing bowl. Discard the lemongrass tops but keep the water.

Pound the fish until fluffy. Alternatively use a fork or potato masher to mash the fish.

Add all curry paste ingredients into a blender and add just enough of the coconut milk to blend. Blend until smooth.

Pour the curry paste into the pot with the fish poaching water in it.

Add the remaining coconut milk, fish sauce, palm sugar, tamarind, and the pounded fish.

Simmer the curry for 15-20 minutes to reduce, longer if needed if the sauce looks too thin.

While the curry simmers, cook the noodles by boiling them in hot water, 7-10 mins depending on the size and brand. They should be tender all the way through but not mushy. Drain the noodles and place into a bowl of cold water.

Once cool enough to handle, pick up a bunch of noodles with your fingers and curl them into a bundle on a folded paper towel. Repeat with the remaining noodles and make several little bundles - these bundles are for easy portioning.

Serve the noodles with the accompaniments, and ladle the sauce over them. Mix it all up and enjoy!

## Advance Prep Tips

Here are various things you can do to make this dish weeknight friendly.

Make the curry paste in advance and freeze. Blend all the herbs with just enough water to blend, then freeze in a zip top bag. When ready to cook, simply add the frozen paste into the pot of fish poaching water. You may need to increase simmering time a bit as you'll have more liquid to cook off.

The finished sauce can be made in advance and freeze. On serving day simply cook the noodles, thaw the sauce, and it's ready to go!

The noodles can be cooked ahead of time and refrigerated for a few days. To reheat, simply blanch them in boiling water for 5 seconds or microwave to reheat.

The noodles can be soaked and rehydrated in advance, drain and kept in the fridge for a few days. This will cut cooking time down significantly. Soaked noodles take only 2-3 minutes to cook through.

## Storing and Reheating Leftovers

Cooked noodles will last in the fridge for up to a week. Microwave until steaming hot to reheat, or dip them into boiling water for literally 3 seconds to warm up; since the noodles are already cooked you do not want them to be cooked in the water for too long during the reheating process.

The finished curry sauce will last in the fridge for about a week. It can also be frozen.

Phitsanulok sits on the main Bangkok–Chiang Mai railway line and has a regional airport, which makes it an easy stop on a northbound Thailand itinerary. Many travelers pass through on their way to Sukhothai without spending the night. On our Central Thailand route —from Kamphaeng Phet to Sukhothai—we chose to pause here for a couple of days instead of treating it as a transfer point.

The city is home to one of Thailand’s most revered Buddha images, the Phra Buddha Chinnarat at Wat Phra Si Rattana Mahathat, often considered second in importance only to the Emerald Buddha in Bangkok. Nearby are Sukhothai-era remains such as Wat Wihan Thong, with its towering standing Buddha. This Phitsanulok guide shows you how the city offers not only temple grounds but also a look at everyday Thai life, night markets, a peculiar lunch spot and a trendy bar.

## Is Phitsanulok Worth Visiting? How Long Should You Stay?

Phitsanulok makes the most sense for travelers already exploring Central Thailand’s historic corridor. If you are moving between Bangkok and Sukhothai and have an interest in Buddhist art, you will not regret it.

Plan for a two-night stay. That gives you a full day to cover the main stops in our Phitsanulok guide—temples, ruins, and local experiences.
## Phitsanulok Itinerary: Temples, Ruins & Local Markets in One Day

We structured our walking tour of Phitsanulok from east to west, starting at Wat Aranyik and finishing at Wat Wihan Thong. The route connects Sukhothai-era ruins with the city’s main temple complex and follows the Nan River for part of the way. It’s a manageable walk that combines historic sites, a riverside stretch, and time for lunch at one of the city’s most popular local spots.
## Wat Aranyik in Phitsanulok: A Forest Temple in Ruins

We start at one of the lesser-frequented sights in Phitsanulok. Despite sitting on the edge of a sizeable city, you can’t help but feel a bit like Indiana Jones walking through the site. The area is thick with overgrown vegetation, broken brick, and uneven laterite foundations. The complex has a rough, almost neglected appearance.

These ruins date back to the 14th-century Sukhothai Kingdom and are surrounded by a moat. At the center stands the remains of a large chedi, with scattered terraces, red brick walls, and fragments of Buddha images typical of the period. I’m glad we visited, but if you’re already temple-saturated from Sukhothai or Kamphaeng Phet, you could reasonably skip the one-kilometer walk and start with the next stop on this itinerary.
## Wat Chedi Yot Thong: The Last Lotus-Bud Stupa in Phitsanulok

Walking down Phaya Suea Road—a typical suburban stretch lined with small shops and markets—you turn onto a side street and reach Wat Chedi Yot Thong. The surviving lotus-bud chedi dates to the Sukhothai period and is believed to have been built around the same time as Wat Phra Si Rattana Mahathat. Scholars note that both temples originally featured lotus-bud–style structures, before Wat Phra Si Rattana Mahathat was later modified into its present prang form.
## Wat Phra Si Rattana Mahathat: Home of the Phra Buddha Chinnarat

Wat Phra Si Rattana Mahathat (also known as Wat Yai or Woramahawihan) is a large temple complex, and we enter through its eastern gate. The temple dates to 1357, during the Sukhothai era, and remains the city’s principal religious site and an obligatory stop in any itinerary of the city.
### The Golden Prang and the Standing Buddha Phra Attharot

As you cross the eastern gate, the golden prang and the standing Buddha, Phra Attharot, come into view. You reach them through a landscaped courtyard with lotus ponds and the brick foundations of a former viharn.

Phra Attharot dates to the 14th century and stands about 18 meters high. Rendered in the distinctive Sukhothai style, with elongated proportions and a flame-like ushnisha, the image is shown in the Abhaya, or “have no fear,” gesture. It remains an active object of devotion, with locals continuing to pay respects and make offerings.

Behind it stands the central prang, believed to enshrine sacred Buddha relics within its core. The structure has undergone later modifications, but its base reflects earlier Sukhothai influences before being reshaped into its present Khmer-inspired tower form. On the eastern side, a narrow and notably steep staircase leads to a small upper chamber that is closed to visitors. The other sides feature recessed niches housing golden standing Buddha images.
### Phra Buddha Chinnarat: Thailand’s Revered Sukhothai Buddha

After admiring the prang, continue toward the western main entrance. The ornate façade, decorated with mother-of-pearl inlay, marks the approach to the vihara (assembly hall) . The hall is crowded with local worshippers paying their respects and posing for photographs, with professional photographers offering their services just outside.

Inside sits the Phra Buddha Chinnarat, often translated as the “King of Victory,” and widely regarded as the second most revered Buddha image in Thailand after the Emerald Buddha. Cast in bronze in the 14th century, the image stands about 3.75 meters high and is depicted in the subduing Mara posture. Two companion images were cast at the same time and are now housed at Wat Bowonniwet in Bangkok (as described in our [Khaosan](https://www.trailblazingadventures.com/khao-san-road-bangkok/) blog) . The Buddha with the flame-like aureole projects calm and reassurance to the devotees that compete to get close, some for professional photos, others for quiet meditation.
### The Cloister Galleries

Leave the vihara through the front-left door and enter the surrounding cloister. The covered galleries hold an extensive collection of seated Buddha images arranged along the walls, interspersed with small altars and more elaborate display groupings. There is a section that presents archaeological fragments recovered from the temple grounds.
## Where to Have Lunch in Phitsanulok

After visiting Wat Phra Si Rattana Mahathat, it’s time for lunch. Luckily, one of the most popular spots in Phitsanulok is just a short stroll away along the Nan River: [Hoi Kha Rim Nan Noodle](https://maps.app.goo.gl/pmo7GY2LUUzar4cf8) Restaurant

The place is packed — because bloggers, and just about every other TikTok on Phitsanulok, mention it. In this case, the attention is justified. Sit on the stools facing the street and your feet dangle over the edge — the name of the restaurant literally means ‘ dangling legs by the Nan River. ’ It’s informal, busy, and very local.

Order the Sukhothai-style Yom Tum noodles. You choose your noodle type — thin rice noodles, wide rice noodles, egg noodles, even glass noodles. I went for the traditional version with thin rice noodles. The broth leans sweet and tangy, finished with crispy fried noodles on top. If you want to go the full decadent route like I did, add an egg. Prices stay firmly local despite the attention — for about 4 USD, you get a full meal.
## Chan Royal Palace Historical Center and King Naresuan Shrine

After lunch, walk upriver toward the Elephant Bridge — named for the large elephant statue on its western side. Just across the bridge lies the Chan Royal Palace Historical Center, a spacious park built on the site of the former royal residence of Phitsanulok.

Today, the complex includes a reconstructed palace building that functions as a museum dedicated to King Naresuan the Great, one of Thailand’s most revered historical figures. Born in Phitsanulok in the 16th century, Naresuan is remembered for declaring independence from Burma and consolidating the Ayutthaya Kingdom.

North of the museum stands the shrine to King Naresuan, housing a life-size image of the king and still actively venerated by locals who bring flowers, incense, and symbolic offerings. What immediately catches the eye are the countless rooster statues placed around the shrine. The rooster is tied to a well-known legend from Naresuan’s youth, when he reportedly won a cockfight against a Burmese prince while living as a royal hostage. The story came to symbolize courage and resolve, and today devotees leave rooster figures in homage to that early act of defiance and an offering believed to bring good fortune.
## Exploring Wat Wihan Thong: Ancient Ruins and a Majestic Standing Buddha

The temple sits within a small historical park, now a green space where locals jog or stroll in the evenings. Wat Wihan Thong dates to the 15th century, during the Ayutthaya period. What remains today are the imposing stone columns and the brick base of the former vihara. Rising behind them stands a contemporary Sukhothai-style standing Buddha — a replica of the original image that once stood there.
## An Evening in Phitsanulok: Night Market and Craft Beer

In the evening we headed to the Phitsanulok Railway Station Night Market, a one-block stretch lined with stalls selling ready-made dishes. The food looked good, but there were no stools or seating. Most people seemed to stop by after work, picking up dinner to take home rather than eating on site.

Afterwards, we walked one block south to Phayalithai Street, a popular strip with restaurants and bars. We stopped at a specialty beer bar – [Girls don’t cry](https://maps.app.goo.gl/99pBrSuMNdFHoWzB7) – featuring local craft breweries. I went for a pilsner called Mojo Mojo. At around 10 USD for a pint, it’s expensive by Thai standards — but it was a well crafted pint along good service and good music.

## How to Get to Phitsanulok and How to Get Around

Phitsanulok functions as a transportation hub in Central Thailand. You can fly from Bangkok’s Don Mueang Airport to Phitsanulok in about an hour on a low-cost carrier. The city also sits on Thailand’s Northern Line, making the Bangkok to Phitsanulok train a straightforward option if you’re heading toward Chiang Mai or coming south. Long-distance buses connect the city to other destinations across the lower north as well.

Within town, getting around is simple. Songthaews circulate near the main sights, and both Grab and Bolt operate reliably for short rides. Most places in this Phitsanulok guide are a quick drive from the train station area, which makes logistics easy.
## Is Phitsanulok Worth Visiting? Final Thoughts

We’re drawn to medium-sized Thai cities like Phitsanulok. Fewer tourists, excellent food, and genuine hospitality. Add Sukhothai-era ruins, active temple grounds, and the commanding presence of the Phra Buddha Chinnarat, and it becomes more than a transit stop.

If you’re planning a Central Thailand itinerary between [Bangkok](https://www.trailblazingadventures.com/3-days-in-bangkok-itinerary/) and Sukhothai, Phitsanulok makes sense as a two-night addition rather than a quick transfer. It sits naturally within a broader UNESCO corridor that takes you through the walled ruins of [Kamphaeng Phet](https://www.trailblazingadventures.com/kamphaeng-phet/), the temples of [Sukhothai](https://www.trailblazingadventures.com/sukhothai-itinerary/), and the quieter remains at [Si Satchanalai](https://www.trailblazingadventures.com/si-satchanalai/) — all covered in our Central [Thailand](https://www.trailblazingadventures.com/asia/thailand/) guides. From Phitsanulok you can also head north into the mountains toward the extraordinary [Wat Phra That Pha Sorn Kaew](https://www.trailblazingadventures.com/wat-pha-sorn-kaew/). This Phitsanulok guide should give you a clear framework for how to structure that stop.

## Dominant intent and owner boundary

The page owns **Gai Tod Hat Yai / Hat Yai fried chicken** as a traveller-first southern Thai fried-chicken style: marinated chicken with a relatively thin crisp coating or skin, strongly associated with fried shallots and commonly eaten with sticky rice. Garlic, coriander root or stems, pepper, soy or fish sauce and sugar are familiar; cumin, coriander seed, coconut milk, exact coating and dipping sauce vary.

Keep these independent:

- Generic Gai Tod / Thai fried chicken without the Hat Yai identity.
- Khao Man Gai Tod: crispy chicken served inside the Thai chicken-rice plate with its own rice, soup and sauce system.
- Gai Yang: grilled chicken rather than fried chicken.
- Khao Mok Gai: southern Thai chicken biryani with spiced rice and sauce.
- Korean, American, Japanese, Malaysian or Indonesian fried chicken and restaurant/delivery intent.
- Recipe-only, calorie, “best in the world” and health intent.

## Evidence-backed information gain

1. **Fried shallot is the strongest identity signal.** Two independent Thai specialists treat crispy fried shallots as central to the Hat Yai name even while their marinades and coatings differ.
2. **The crust is deliberately lighter.** A current Hat Yai-born specialist contrasts the thin rice-flour coating with a thick craggy American-style crust. Other documented versions build a thicker batter, so “thin” is typical rather than universal.
3. **Spices are not a fixed authenticity test.** One hometown version explicitly omits cumin and coriander seed; another current southern-Thai recipe includes both. Garlic, coriander root/stem and pepper are more stable aromatic signals.
4. **Sticky rice is the service anchor, not part of the coating.** Sources repeatedly pair the chicken and fried shallots with glutinous rice; sweet chilli sauce or som tam are familiar accompaniments but optional.
5. **Rice flour does not guarantee gluten-free.** Soy sauce, sweet soy, seasoning, baking powder, premix, cross-contact and a shared fryer can introduce wheat/gluten even when the visible coating is rice-flour based.
6. **Hat Yai style is not automatic halal proof.** A current official Central Islamic Council result demonstrates that product certification has a specific holder and expiry. Verify the vendor’s current certification, chicken source, sauces, marinade and shared oil rather than inferring status from city or dish name.

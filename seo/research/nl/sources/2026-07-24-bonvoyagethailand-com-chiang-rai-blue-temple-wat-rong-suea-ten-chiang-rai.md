# Blue Temple Chiang Rai (Wat Rong Suea Ten)
## How Is the Blue Temple Different from the White Temple?
| Temple | Color | Represents | Artist |
|---|
| White Temple | White | Heaven, purity | Chalermchai Kositpipat |
| Blue Temple | Blue | Wisdom, dharma | Putha Kabkaew |
| Black House | Black | Mortality, earthly desires | Thawan Duchanee |

The Blue Temple differs from the White Temple in color, meaning, and maker: the White Temple (by Chalermchai Kositpipat) represents heaven and purity, while the Blue Temple (by his student Putha Kabkaew) embodies wisdom and dharma. Visiting both in one day creates a contrast that neither offers alone.

The White Temple represents heaven and enlightenment, while the Blue Temple embodies wisdom, dharma, and the path to overcoming material desire. Visiting both in one day creates a powerful contrast that neither offers alone.

The Black House (Baan Dam), however, is not a temple but an art museum exploring themes of death and the cycle of life. Together, these three sites are known locally as the “Colors of Chiang Rai.”

The connection: Putha Kabkaew studied under Chalermchai Kositpipat, the creator of the White Temple. The Blue Temple is often called the “younger sibling” – it shares a similar artistic vision but conveys a different message.

## Why Is the Blue Temple Blue?

The Blue Temple is painted deep sapphire blue because the color symbolizes purity, wisdom, and the Buddhist principle of letting go of material attachments. Every surface, walls, pillars, roof, and statues, carries the blue, offset by gleaming gold accents.

In a country where temples usually gleam with gold, that single artistic decision is what made Wat Rong Suea Ten famous.
## Is the Blue Temple Worth Visiting?

Yes, the Blue Temple is free to enter, takes 45 minutes to an hour to see, and sits 10 minutes from Chiang Rai city center, making it one of the easiest of Chiang Rai’s signature sights to include in any itinerary. It also stays noticeably calmer than the White Temple, even in peak season (November–February).

Most visitors see it in one full day together with the [White Temple](https://www.bonvoyagethailand.com/chiang-rai/white-temple/), [Black House](https://www.bonvoyagethailand.com/chiang-rai/black-house-baan-dam-chiang-rai/), and [Wat Huay Pla Kang](https://www.bonvoyagethailand.com/chiang-rai/wat-huay-pla-kang-chiang-rai/).
## History: Temple of the Dancing Tiger

The Blue Temple’s Thai name, Wat Rong Suea Ten, translates to “Temple of the Dancing Tiger.” According to local lore, tigers once roamed the abandoned temple grounds nearly a century ago. Villagers saw them leaping across the overgrown ruins, which led to the name “dancing tigers.”

The original ancient temple had fallen into disrepair. In 1996, the local community decided to rebuild. According to [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wat_Rong_Suea_Ten), the modern Blue Temple was redesigned and completed in 2016 by local artist Putha Kabkaew, a student of Chalermchai Kositpipat (creator of the White Temple). Like many sacred spaces in Thailand, it’s considered a work-in-progress, with artists continually adding details.

### The Exterior

The first thing you notice is the color – not just blue, but a specific shade that seems to shift between sapphire and cerulean depending on the light. The entire structure is adorned with:

Naga serpents – mythical snake guardians whose scales wind up the main staircase with mesmerizing detail

Angel figures – winged protectors positioned at windows, staffs held behind their backs

Yaksha guardians – brightly colored nature spirits keeping watch against negative energies

Standing Buddha – a large white Buddha statue behind the main hall in the abhaya mudra pose (the gesture of fearlessness and protection)

The gold accents catch sunlight throughout the day, creating different moods, from early morning softness to late afternoon warmth.

The stairs to the main entrance are guarded by naga serpents, whose tails ripple with detailed scales and twist and whirl around in entrancing elegance. The temple windows are protected by an angelic figure, posing proudly on a pedestal with feathered wings spread wide, a staff grasped behind its back. Brightly colored Yakshas, or female nature spirits, wait nearby to keep the temple safe from evil.

Just behind the temple, a Buddha statue stands in an abaya-mudra pose, emitting an air of calmness and inviting visitors to relinquish fear and anxiety.
### Inside the Blue Temple: The Prayer Hall

Step through the ornate entrance and the interior unfolds as a kaleidoscope of devotion. Every surface is adorned with elaborate murals depicting the Buddha’s life, painted in a contemporary style that feels distinctly modern.

Inside the Blue Temple you’ll find a white porcelain Buddha seated in the bhumisparsha (earth-touching) mudra, surrounded by murals of the Buddha’s life on every wall and a gold geometric ceiling. The white statue appears to glow against the sapphire walls — it’s the image most visitors remember.

The centerpiece: is a large white porcelain Buddha statue seated in the bhumisparsha mudra (earth-touching gesture), representing the moment of enlightenment. The white statue appears almost ethereal against the blue walls, seemingly glowing in the reflected color of the room.

The murals: Look closely at the walls. Scenes from the Buddha’s life are rendered in extraordinary detail and framed in ornate gold. The ceiling is particularly striking, featuring geometric patterns that visitors often describe as almost psychedelic.

The exit: As you leave, the wall around the doorframe depicts Buddhist representations of hell, with the doorway itself forming a demon’s mouth. It serves as a reminder: you are walking back into the material world – the realm of attachment and suffering. Outside, vendors sell blue ice cream (made with butterfly pea flower) on hot days, offering a lighter note after the temple’s spiritual weight.

### What Is the Dress Code at the Blue Temple?

The Blue Temple dress code requires shoulders and knees to be covered, and you remove your shoes before entering the prayer hall. Bring a scarf or sarong if you’re wearing a tank top or shorts — it’s an active place of worship, so speak quietly inside.

## When Is the Best Time to Photograph the Blue Temple?

The best time to photograph the Blue Temple is early morning (7–9 AM) or late afternoon (4–6 PM), when golden-hour light makes the gold accents glow. Overcast days are a bonus, the blue reads richer and more saturated without harsh shadows.

The Blue Temple is one of the most photogenic temples in Thailand, but getting good shots requires good timing:

Golden hour: The gold accents glow beautifully at sunrise and sunset.

Overcast days: The blue appears richer and more saturated without harsh shadows.

Interior shots: A wide-angle lens (or phone setting) captures the murals better.

Avoid midday: Harsh shadows and bright sun wash out the blue color.

Our guides’ tip: The standing Buddha statue behind the main hall is less crowded than the front entrance and makes for striking photos with fewer people in the frame.
